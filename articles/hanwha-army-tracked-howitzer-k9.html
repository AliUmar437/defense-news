Hanwha Eyes Army's Tracked Howitzer Program After Wheeled-Arty Win

James CarterOct 09, 2026 14:09 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

South Korea's Hanwha is not done winning American artillery contracts. Weeks after the Army tapped its wheeled K9 howitzer for a prototype program, the company says it will enter the service's new competition for a tracked variant — offering either its K9A1 or K9A2 self-propelled guns.

"I think it's a must. I mean, when we had early conversations with the Army, we actually thought the Army might be procuring tracked [variants] first," said Mike Smith, Hanwha USA's chief operating officer and president of Land Systems.

A menu of tracked options

Hanwha USA is looking at a "menu of options" for the tracked competition, Smith said. The K9A1 is the widely fielded and most cost-effective choice, while the newer K9A2 brings more automation and firepower. According to Hanwha, the K9A1 mounts a 155mm, 52-caliber cannon with a range of over 40 kilometers, firing six to eight rounds per minute for three minutes at full capacity.

The K9A2 is the company's next step. It keeps the same drive power as the K9A1 but adds a fully automated turret capable of nine to ten rounds per minute, two independent loading arms for projectiles and propellants, and a new autoloader system that raises the rate of fire while cutting the crew size. Both are members of the K9 Thunder family, one of the most successful artillery systems in the world — in service with South Korea and exported to Poland, Norway, Finland, Australia, Romania, and others.

The Army laid the groundwork for the competition on Sept. 30, when its Program Acquisition Executive for Fires posted a request for information seeking tracked howitzers and a companion resupply vehicle. The solicitation asked vendors about production lead times, maximum production capacity, and strategies for moving production to the United States if their facilities are overseas. An industry day for interested vendors was scheduled for the following week.

Smith suggested Hanwha's existing production lines could shorten the wait: several K9 variants are already in service with land forces around the world. The howitzers are also "completely tailorable," he added — Hanwha can tweak its vehicles to meet the Army's formal requirements.

The wheeled win that set the stage

Hanwha's bid for the tracked program follows its victory in the wheeled competition, where it beat an unusually crowded field. The Army awarded Hanwha Defense USA a deal worth up to $233 million for the accelerated delivery of six wheeled prototype howitzers, with an option for 12 more. Soldiers will use the prototypes — known as Mobile Tactical Cannon systems — in operational experiments to assess performance, reliability, and supportability in realistic combat conditions.

The losers were a who's-who of global artillery: an Elbit America team with Anduril and Oshkosh Defense offering the SIGMA platform, Leonardo DRS with KNDS and a Caesar-based gun, Germany's Rheinmetall with its Remote Controlled Howitzer 155, and Britain's BAE Systems with Archer.

Hanwha is already laying industrial groundwork in the United States. The company signed a three-year lease on an idle automobile plant in Opelika, Alabama, in April as an integration and test facility for the howitzer program, and Korean media estimate a future mass-production contract — planned for 2027, with initial fielding in 2030 — could reach about 500 guns worth roughly $7.1 billion.

Why the Army wants both

An Army spokesperson told Breaking Defense the service is pursuing the two variants separately because they "address different operational requirements and formations." The wheeled howitzer is meant to give formations equipped with towed artillery more mobility, survivability, and responsiveness. The tracked variant is intended to keep up with armored formations. Both are aimed at replacing the towed M777 — some of which are 30 to 40 years old — and the self-propelled M109A7, a design first fielded in the 1960s.

The urgency comes from Ukraine, where massed tube artillery has remained decisive even in an age of drones and precision missiles. The Army wants guns that shoot farther, fire faster, and move before enemy counter-battery radar can answer — lessons written in Ukraine that Hanwha's K9, with its automation and rapid-fire rate, was built to deliver.
