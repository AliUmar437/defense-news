Lockheed Unveils PAC-3 Edge, Its Next-Generation Patriot Interceptor

Robert HayesOct 09, 2026 19:45 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Lockheed Martin has unveiled the PAC-3 Edge, its next-generation Patriot interceptor — and is backing the missile with hundreds of millions of dollars of its own money before the Army has even settled on the final requirement.

The missile, revealed Oct. 9 just ahead of the Association of the U.S. Army's annual conference, is Lockheed's bid for the Army's Future Interceptor program, which seeks a successor to the current PAC-3 Missile Segment Enhancement (MSE). Tim Cahill, president of Lockheed's missiles and fire control division, told Breaking Defense the company was one of two firms selected to advance in the program. He declined to name the other competitor, and the Army did not respond to a request for comment.

What Edge changes

The PAC-3 is the Patriot system's hit-to-kill interceptor, designed to destroy incoming ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and aircraft by slamming into them directly rather than exploding nearby. It has seen real combat in the Middle East and Ukraine, where Patriot batteries have become some of the most valuable — and most heavily targeted — air defense assets in the world.

Edge promises to take that performance further. "PAC-3 Edge will be longer ranged. It'll be higher altitude. It'll be more capable to take on targets that are trying to hide," Cahill said. "It'll have greater sensitivity in its seeker. It'll have greater capability to maneuver and to respond to threats."

The missile pairs a new in-house seeker with a multi-pulse solid rocket motor developed with Northrop Grumman. The seeker was designed by Lockheed's Strigo Product Center, and the company says the motor's pulsed thrust gives the interceptor greater range and altitude throughout its flight than the legacy PAC-3. Control-system upgrades are aimed at delivering that performance against maneuvering targets, including the hypersonic weapons that are stressing current interceptors.

Lockheed plans to begin testing the missile in 2027, with a first deployment targeted for 2031. The company displayed a full-scale model of the weapon at AUSA.

Lockheed's big bet

The scale of Lockheed's own investment is the real story. The company has already put between $50 million and $100 million into the missile itself, Cahill said — and it intends to spend hundreds of millions more. The seeker effort is bigger still: Lockheed plans to invest about $800 million in the seeker, having already spent "several" hundred million dollars on it, and intends to apply the technology across both offensive and defensive systems.

That confidence is not without a business case. Demand for air defense is at historic highs, driven by conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East, and stockpiles are strained. In January, Lockheed signed a seven-year framework agreement with the Pentagon to expand PAC-3 manufacturing capacity from about 600 interceptors a year to 2,000. In July, the Army awarded the company a contract worth up to $58.6 billion to ramp up PAC-3 production, Reuters reported. Cahill told Reuters that the Edge would cost roughly the same as the current PAC-3 MSE, with an annual production rate of about 2,000 units at full production.

Edge would be built on Lockheed's existing PAC-3 line in Camden, Arkansas. The company is building a new MSE production facility there equipped with robotics and advanced manufacturing automation, with production set to move into the new plant — and the legacy facility to receive the same upgrades. "Before we ever get to the point where we're producing Edge, we will have already outfitted the entire factory from end to end with the most modern manufacturing capability available," Cahill said.

A program that keeps coming back

The Future Interceptor program follows a fits-and-starts effort by the Army to develop a next-generation Patriot interceptor. In 2024, the service announced plans to cancel the Lower-Tier Future Interceptor (LTFI) program, which had seen Raytheon and Lockheed competing to build the new missile. A year later, an Army official suggested the program could move forward after all.

Earlier this year, Lockheed also announced the PAC-3 ACE, a less capable and lower-cost version of the MSE intended for international customers. Edge is the opposite play: the highest-performance interceptor Lockheed can build for the hardest targets the Army expects to face.
