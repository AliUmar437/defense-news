Coast Guard Orders Its First MQ-9B Drones in $248 Million Deal

David SullivanOct 09, 2026 20:10 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

The U.S. Coast Guard has ordered its first MQ-9B drones, buying four of the long-range unmanned aircraft for $247.8 million in a move that gives the maritime service its own persistent airborne eyes for the first time.

The purchase, announced Oct. 9, covers four General Atomics aircraft along with ground control stations and supporting equipment, bought through a U.S. Customs and Border Protection contract. The aircraft are not expected to carry weapons, a service spokesperson told Breaking Defense. They will support offshore surveillance, search-and-rescue operations, and counter-narcotics missions — a broad charter for a service that patrols enormous areas with a relatively small fleet of cutters and aircraft.

From borrowed drones to its own fleet

The Coast Guard is no stranger to the MQ-9, but until now it has always flown someone else's. The service has partnered with CBP since the mid-2000s to operate the border agency's MQ-9A drones, mostly out of Texas. According to a Coast Guard factsheet, those borrowed aircraft helped CBP seize 60,700 pounds of illegal drugs and catch roughly 20 smugglers in fiscal year 2026 alone.

Owning its own airframes changes the equation. "The MQ-9B long-range unmanned aircraft systems will expand our reach and strengthen our ability to protect the nation," said Anthony Antognoli, the assistant commandant and program executive officer for robotics and autonomous systems, in the Coast Guard's announcement. "This investment will give Coast Guard crews a longer-range view of our maritime domain, helping them detect threats earlier, make better-informed decisions and act faster across our missions."

The funding traces back to the FY25 reconciliation package — the "One Big Beautiful Bill" signed into law in July 2025 — which directed nearly $25 billion to the Coast Guard, along with FY26 appropriations. The service first revealed its MQ-9 plans last year, when Military Times reported that about $266 million of the reconciliation money was earmarked for the drones.

What four drones buy

The MQ-9B is the long-range variant of the Reaper family, and its numbers explain the Coast Guard's interest. General Atomics says the SeaGuardian version is designed to conduct maritime intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance missions lasting more than 30 hours at a time, with a mission radius of roughly 1,200 nautical miles. For a service whose ships can take days to reach a distant smuggling corridor, that kind of persistence — a single aircraft watching an area for a day and a half, then handing off to a relief crew without missing a beat — is hard to get any other way.

That endurance compares favorably with what the Coast Guard flies today. A Coast Guard C-130 patrols for about 14 hours at a stretch, with a small crew working continuously throughout. With the MQ-9, crews rotate roughly every two hours, keeping a fresh set of eyes on the sensor feed while the aircraft stays on station.

The SeaGuardian variant is purpose-built for over-water work, with maritime radar, automatic identification system receivers, and the ability to dispense and monitor sonobuoys for anti-submarine missions. The Coast Guard did not say whether its aircraft will be the SeaGuardian version, and a service spokesperson did not answer that question, according to Defense Daily.

A bigger aviation build-up

The drone buy is one piece of a much larger Coast Guard aviation recapitalization. FlightGlobal reported the service also ordered six new C-130J transports as part of a roughly $3 billion effort to modernize its air fleet, with the aircraft positioned as long-range maritime patrol platforms in their own right.

For a service long accustomed to making do with hand-me-downs and borrowed time on other agencies' drones, the MQ-9B order marks a step toward persistent, unmanned surveillance across America's maritime approaches. The drones will detect, classify, and track activity well before it reaches the homeland — giving cutters and boarding teams the one thing they can never get enough of: time.
