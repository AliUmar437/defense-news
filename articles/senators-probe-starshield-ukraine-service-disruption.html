Senators Demand Pentagon Probe After Ukraine's Starshield Service Was Cut Mid-Battle

Michael ReynoldsOct 16, 2026 12:42 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Nine bipartisan senators have called for an immediate Pentagon inspector general investigation into the sudden disruption of Ukraine's Starshield satellite service, a breakdown they say endangered American personnel and left Ukrainian forces exposed during Russian air attacks.

Senators Jeanne Shaheen, the top Democrat on the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, and Thom Tillis of North Carolina led the letter, sent Oct. 15 to Defense Department Inspector General Platte Moring III. It was signed by Dick Durbin, Susan Collins, Jack Reed, John Cornyn, Chris Coons, Lisa Murkowski and Richard Blumenthal — a coalition spanning both parties.

The senators say reported disruptions between Oct. 12 and 14 cut into the communications Ukrainian drones and aircraft depend on. They asked Moring to determine whether U.S. officials approved of, directed, or had advance knowledge of the restrictions, and whether Pentagon decisions contributed to the dangerous circumstances.

"We are particularly alarmed by incidents in which U.S. diplomatic, military, and security personnel were unable to reach designated bunkers during Russian air raids," the senators wrote, citing reported interruptions to intelligence-sharing and early-warning systems.

Ukrainian military testing on Oct. 14 found Starlink terminals operating above roughly 63 miles per hour were being restricted, according to the senators' account. For a country whose front-line strike and interceptor drones depend on satellite connectivity, even a temporary cap amounts to a blindfold.

A White House order, officials say

The letter landed a day after Reuters reported, citing two U.S. officials and a Western official, that the order to slow Ukraine's Starshield service came from inside the White House. Leaders at the Defense Department received word that the Starshield service for Ukraine's military needed to be throttled, the officials said, though they did not identify who issued the directive. A senior U.S. official denied that the president personally made the request, and the White House did not answer questions about whether other administration officials did.

Elon Musk, the SpaceX chief executive, said on X that because Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the U.S. government, decisions about speed and usage limits are made by the U.S. military or the president. The Pentagon is the biggest customer of Starshield, the military-grade version of Starlink.

Pressure on Kyiv over refinery strikes

The disruption came as relations between Washington and Kyiv deteriorated over Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries. President Donald Trump has pressed Ukraine to stop the attacks, blaming them for rising energy costs ahead of November's midterm elections. At a Miami meeting on Oct. 9, his son-in-law Jared Kushner and envoy Steve Witkoff took a hard line with Ukrainian officials, while the U.S. delegation defended a deal for Russia to supply more than 2.25 million barrels of diesel to global markets.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called that deal "weak." Trump, in turn, has called for elections in Ukraine and for Zelenskyy's ouster. American officials have privately threatened to curtail intelligence-sharing about Russian missile launches, a lifeline Ukraine uses to blunt incoming attacks. Kyiv was told the Starshield restriction was a White House decision, a senior Ukrainian official said.

Why it matters

This episode exposes how thoroughly modern warfare now runs through a single company's network. Starshield was built for governments — the Pentagon has spent billions on Starlink and Starshield services, dedicated Starshield satellites, and a spy constellation for the National Reconnaissance Office. That concentration made the service a lever, and the lever was pulled in the middle of a bombing campaign.

For allies watching from elsewhere, the lesson is uncomfortable: dependence on a U.S.-controlled commercial network means battlefield connectivity can become an instrument of political pressure. European defense ministries have quietly begun looking for alternatives, and this incident will accelerate that search.

The inspector general investigation, meanwhile, has a narrow but consequential mandate — to establish who knew what and when. If the order came from within the White House without formal coordination with the Pentagon, the episode raises questions about who, exactly, is authorized to degrade an ally's wartime communications mid-conflict. Whatever Moring finds, the damage to trust between Washington and Kyiv — and between the Pentagon and its contractors — will take longer to repair than a software setting.
