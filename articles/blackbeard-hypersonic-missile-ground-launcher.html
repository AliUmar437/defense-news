Army Shows 'Blackbeard' Hypersonic Missile Firing from Ground Launcher

Michael ReynoldsOct 08, 2026 16:32 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Castelion, the defense startup developing the Pentagon's low-cost hypersonic missile, has released what appears to be the first image of its Blackbeard weapon being fired from a ground launcher, offering a new look at the containerized launch architecture the U.S. Army is pursuing.

The photograph, posted by the company on X, shows a Blackbeard missile leaving a launcher mounted on a high-mobility truck chassis — apparently the converted Oshkosh MKR18 truck the company has used in earlier testing. While Blackbeard test vehicles are now a familiar sight, the image is the first public glimpse of how Castelion intends to package and operate the ground-launched version of the weapon, known as Blackbeard GL.

An affordable hypersonic for the Army

The Army's interest in Blackbeard is rooted in cost as much as performance. The service wants an affordable, mass-produced hypersonic precision-fire capability for time-sensitive, moving, and hardened targets — with a stated objective of roughly 80 percent of the planned Precision Strike Missile (PrSM) Increment 4's capability at a substantially lower cost per round.

The concept is to treat a hypersonic weapon more like a conventional tactical munition than a strategic asset. Existing U.S. hypersonic systems such as the Army's Dark Eagle Long Range Hypersonic Weapon rely on large, highly specialized launch equipment. Blackbeard is instead being built around a containerized launcher compatible with the existing Multiple Launch Rocket System Family of Munitions (MFOM) ecosystem — and eventually the M142 HIMARS fire-control system — so the Army can add a hypersonic effect without a separate launcher, crew, transport, and reload architecture.

The Army's plan calls for an initial ground-launch demonstration using a modified MFOM pod, followed by Blackbeard GL prototypes and a dedicated launcher pod, culminating in a minimum-viable-product flight test using HIMARS fire control. The Army's Fiscal Year 2027 budget documents list a live-fire launch of a "production representative" Blackbeard GL and launcher pod among the program's milestones.

What is known — and what is not

Castelion unveiled the weapon's current external configuration earlier this year: a two-stage arrangement with a booster and an upper vehicle consistent with a hypersonic glide or maneuvering payload. The ground-launch model lacks the cruciform tailfins seen on the air-launched version. The precise propulsion system and flight profile remain undisclosed, and little is known about its performance parameters.

The company has hinted the missile could strike targets just under 500 miles away, though ground launch — without an aircraft's altitude and speed — would likely shorten that. The pictured launcher uses a compact enclosed container; The War Zone estimates at least two and perhaps as many as four missiles would fit a single launcher. Castelion has stressed that its test hardware is deliberately designed for rapid development cycles and should not necessarily be taken as representative of the final weapon.

It is also worth noting what remains unproven: analysts have pointed out that it is not yet certain whether Blackbeard is a true hypersonic glide vehicle or something closer to a maneuvering ballistic missile — a distinction the company has not fully resolved publicly.

A Pentagon bet on mass production

Behind the program sits an unusually concrete Pentagon commitment. In May, the Defense Department announced that once Castelion completes testing and validation, it will award a two-year multi-year procurement contract for a minimum of 500 Blackbeard missiles per year, with options extending up to five years — and that it is seeking authorization and appropriations to buy more than 12,000 Blackbeard missiles over five years.

The Navy is moving in parallel. In April, Castelion received a $105 million Navy contract supporting Blackbeard's integration onto the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet, followed by a $23.4 million order covering 50 early-operational-capability pre-production missiles. A pair of Blackbeard missiles flew on a Super Hornet during a developmental test flight on Sept. 18, 2026. The weapon could eventually reach Navy ships and submarines as well.

The broader push reflects a shift toward lower-cost precision weapons that can be manufactured simply and at scale — increasingly with non-traditional contractors. Castelion, founded by former SpaceX engineers, says it has completed more than 20 developmental flights and is expanding its own production facilities to meet the expected demand.
