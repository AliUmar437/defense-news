Drone Strikes Sink Merchant Ship off Bulgaria as NATO Ally Accelerates Anti-Drone Push

Michael ReynoldsOct 14, 2026 12:41 UTC 3 min readRegion: Europe

SOFIA, Bulgaria — A drone strike sank a cargo vessel and damaged another off Bulgaria's Black Sea coast overnight Tuesday, leaving 11 sailors missing and prompting the NATO ally to summon Russia's ambassador and accelerate plans to join a new alliance anti-drone initiative.

The two merchant vessels were hit by unmanned aircraft in Bulgarian waters on October 13, according to Bulgarian officials. The Guinea-Bissau-flagged cargo ship HS Dignity sank about 15 nautical miles east of the coastal village of Krapets, close to the Romanian border. Five of its 16 crew members were pulled from the water by other merchant vessels; Bulgarian authorities said 11 sailors — believed by the Interior Ministry to be Syrian and Egyptian nationals — remained missing as search-and-rescue operations resumed Wednesday morning after bad weather forced a suspension overnight.

The second vessel, the Malta-flagged Chelsea-3, was struck by three aerial drones roughly 20 miles off the coastal town of Byala. All 15 crew members — seven Ukrainians and eight Azerbaijanis — were evacuated by the Bulgarian border police and brought to the port of Varna, where the injured received medical treatment. None suffered life-threatening injuries, officials said.

A pattern takes shape

The attacks were the second round of drone strikes on commercial shipping near Bulgaria in a single week. On October 6, unidentified sea and aerial drones sank one merchant ship and set another ablaze roughly 70 nautical miles east of Byala, inside Bulgaria's exclusive economic zone. That earlier strike also left crew members missing and drew condemnation from Sofia.

The recurrence is reshaping how Bulgaria and its allies view the Black Sea. Russia's repeated bombing of Ukrainian ports has pushed Kyiv to route more cargo through Danube river ports and along coastal corridors hugging the shores of NATO members Bulgaria and Romania — exactly the waters now under attack.

Bulgarian President Iliana Iotova identified the sunken vessel as the HS Dignity, which had been sailing toward the Romanian port of Braila with a partial cargo and had previously called at Mostaganem, Algeria, according to vessel-tracking data.

Blame and diplomacy

Bulgaria has stopped short of formally accusing Russia of carrying out the strikes, but Prime Minister Rumen Radev said Tuesday their outcome "benefits the Russian Federation." Russia's ambassador to Sofia, Eleonora Mitrofanova, told the government she had no information from the Russian Defense Ministry about attacks on commercial vessels, while not ruling out what she described as a possible provocation by a third party.

Ukraine's foreign minister blamed Moscow directly, urging partners to tighten sanctions and take what he called decisive action to protect freedom of navigation. Ukraine's military has said it conducted no operations near the attack sites.

NATO's anti-drone answer

A day after the sinking, Bulgarian Defence Minister Dimitar Stoyanov told a parliamentary hearing that Sofia expects to join a NATO anti-drone initiative before the end of October. Bulgaria has asked the alliance and the European Union for assistance in securing its Black Sea waters, which sit on NATO's southeastern flank.

Why it matters

The Black Sea has become the clearest example of how drone warfare is migrating from battlefields to commercial sea lanes. Unmanned aircraft are cheap, deniable, and increasingly able to reach far beyond the front lines — two characteristics that make attribution as politically charged as the strikes themselves. Bulgaria, a NATO member with no border on the Russia-Ukraine war zone, is now absorbing its costs.

The initiative Sofia is joining matters less for what it can do this month than for the precedent it sets: alliance members are beginning to treat counter-drone defense as standing maritime infrastructure rather than a wartime improvisation. If the attacks continue, the pressure will shift from defensive measures to the question NATO has so far avoided — what happens when a member state's waters become a regular hunting ground. The answer will define how seriously the alliance takes freedom of navigation in Europe's backyard.
