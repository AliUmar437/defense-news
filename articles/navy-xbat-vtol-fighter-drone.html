Navy Doubles Down on X-BAT VTOL 'Fighter' Drone

James CarterOct 08, 2026 18:26 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

The U.S. Navy is doubling down on the X-BAT, Shield AI's ambitious vertical-takeoff-and-landing "fighter" drone, with a fresh $150 million funding injection and a flight-test schedule it admits is extraordinarily aggressive.

Shield AI announced Oct. 8 that it had received an additional $150 million from the Pentagon's Defense Exportability Features program, routed through the Navy. The money builds on a $50 million investment the Navy made earlier this year in cooperation with the Defense Innovation Unit. Shield AI has committed a matching amount of its own capital, bringing combined public and private investment in X-BAT to $400 million.

The funding supports the Navy's Runway Independent Maritime Expeditionary Strike (RIMES) program, the service's effort to field a jet-powered, runway-independent strike drone. Shield AI's Armor Harris, senior vice president and general manager of the aircraft division, told The War Zone the company is on track to roll out two full-scale prototypes by the end of October, with flight testing to begin before year's end.

A tail-sitter built around the F110

X-BAT's defining feature is its tail-sitter configuration: the roughly fighter-sized aircraft takes off and lands vertically from its tail, then transitions to conventional winged flight. That design would let it operate from amphibious assault ships, destroyers, expeditionary sea bases, and rough forward airfields — places with no catapults, arresting gear, or runways.

The propulsion system centers on the General Electric F110 engine paired with an Axisymmetric Vectoring Exhaust Nozzle, a thrust-vectoring nozzle developed in the 1990s for an experimental F-16 and kept in storage for decades. Shield AI completed engine light-off and nozzle integration testing with GE Aerospace in July. The nozzle provides pitch and yaw control in vertical flight; roll control comes from bleed air routed through thrusters in the wings.

The company says X-BAT will have a maximum range of about 2,000 nautical miles and a service ceiling near 50,000 feet, with a combat radius it now places closer to 1,000 nautical miles. Internal weapons bays sized like the F-35's, plus two plumbed underwing hardpoints, would let it carry weapons or fuel internally and externally — company renderings have shown it with Joint Standoff Weapons inside and a Long Range Anti-Ship Missile under the wing. The planform was reworked this year from a "cranked kite" layout to an arrowhead-shaped profile.

Shoot-and-scoot operations

The aircraft is only half the system. X-BAT launches and lands on a dedicated launch and recovery vehicle — a self-propelled trailer the aircraft never leaves, even for maintenance. Shield AI is testing the latch-and-unlatch mechanism with an unpowered test article and plans to hang the full vehicle from a crane at its X-Forge 2 test site in Kansas for tethered vertical-flight trials before attempting untethered flights.

The trailer concept is built for survivability. Constantly moving launch vehicles are hard to track and destroy, Harris noted, drawing the comparison to missile transporter-erector-launchers. The company envisions hub-and-spoke operations: squadrons based at airfields with weapons and fuel, aircraft dispersed around the airfield perimeter or to forward arming and refueling points, launching from a golf course, an empty taxiway, or a ship's deck.

At sea, Shield AI envisions dozens of X-BATs operating from amphibious assault ships and expeditionary sea bases — adding carrier-like air wings without taking space from crewed aircraft on carriers. That is the core argument for RIMES, which is separate from the Navy's carrier-based collaborative combat aircraft efforts: it extends naval airpower to ships that cannot host conventional fighters.

An extraordinarily ambitious timeline

Shield AI aims to fly a fully missionized X-BAT in 2028 and begin series production in 2029, targeting roughly 150 aircraft per year by the early 2030s from its planned X-Forge 3 production facility in Des Moines, Washington. RIMES itself targets an early operational capability in 2029, funded through milestone-based payments.

Harris would not name a planned fleet size beyond "hundreds to low thousands." A year of wind tunnel, engine, and thermal testing — plus experience with the smaller V-BAT tail-sitter — has gone into risk reduction. Still, the timeline for reaching operational capability within about three years is, as The War Zone put it, extraordinarily ambitious. The vertical-flight regime that underpins everything X-BAT promises has yet to be demonstrated.
