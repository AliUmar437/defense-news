Lockheed Unveils PAC-3 Edge, Its Bid for the Army's Future Patriot Interceptor

James CarterOct 13, 2026 12:39 UTC 3 min readRegion: North America

WASHINGTON - Lockheed Martin has pulled the wraps off its bid to give the Army a next-generation Patriot interceptor - and its pitch is as much about the price tag as the technology.

The PAC-3 Edge, unveiled ahead of the Association of the U.S. Army's annual conference, is Lockheed's offering for the Army's Future Interceptor program, a competition to develop a follow-on to the current PAC-3 Missile Segment Enhancement (MSE) interceptor. Tim Cahill, president of Lockheed's missiles and fire control unit, told Breaking Defense that the company was one of two firms selected to advance in the program. He declined to name the other competitor, and the Army did not respond to a request for more information.

New seeker, new motor

The Edge builds on the battle-tested PAC-3 MSE. Three upgrades define it: a new company-built seeker, upgraded flight controls, and a multi-pulse solid rocket motor designed by Northrop Grumman. Lockheed says the combination extends the interceptor's reach to higher altitudes and longer ranges, with better sensitivity to pick up difficult targets and better maneuverability to chase them.

"PAC-3 Edge is designed to go after the most stressing targets that we - and, more importantly, that the Department of War - believes are the next-generation threats that they are going to be seeing in the fight from our adversaries," Cahill said in an interview with Breaking Defense, referring to the Pentagon by its new secondary name.

"PAC-3 Edge will be longer ranged. It will be higher altitude. It will be more capable to take on targets that are trying to hide," he added. "It will have greater sensitivity in its seeker. It will have greater capability to maneuver and to respond to threats."

The seeker is built at Lockheed's Strigo Product Center, backed by $250 million in company investment. Cahill said $50 million to $100 million or more has already been spent on the missile, with hundreds of millions more planned, and Lockheed is displaying a full-scale model at the AUSA conference.

A price-driven pitch

The sharpest part of the sales pitch is cost. "The cost of this would be about that of the existing PAC-3 MSE," Cahill told Reuters - roughly $4 million per missile, according to Military Times - removing the exact objection that sank the Army's last attempt at a next-generation Patriot interceptor. Army leaders scrapped that earlier effort in 2024, calling it "very expensive," Breaking Defense reported, before reviving the requirement as the Future Interceptor program.

The timeline is aggressive but framed as company-backed development rather than a Pentagon research effort: test flights targeted for 2027 and residual operational capability by 2031, goals Cahill tied to Lockheed's internal funding. At full production, Cahill told Reuters, output could reach about 2,000 missiles a year - matching the rate the Pentagon agreed in January to lift PAC-3 production toward.

Why it matters

The PAC-3 Edge is the clearest sign yet of how the Pentagon's missile-defense buying has changed. A year ago the pattern was familiar: a new interceptor grew expensive, the Army canceled it, and the requirement died. Now the requirement is back - but only with a contractor willing to build the better missile at the same price and fund the early development itself. That is not Lockheed being generous; it is the industry reading the current Pentagon's demand for cheaper, faster, contractor-invested development.

The unveiling lands amid the biggest interceptor production wave in recent memory. Days earlier the Pentagon awarded Raytheon up to $6.3 billion to keep building SM-3 Block IB interceptors through 2034, and last month Lockheed landed a potential $20.7 billion AMRAAM deal. Across every layer, Washington is signing multiyear deals to lock in production, restock arsenals drawn down by Ukraine and Middle East conflicts, and feed the Golden Dome homeland defense initiative.

What to watch: the identity of the second Future Interceptor competitor, which will say a lot about the Army's tolerance for risk; whether the 2027 test schedule holds, since it underpins the entire company-funded model; and the funding ramp the program is slated to receive beginning in fiscal year 2028 - the moment this stops being Lockheed's pitch and becomes the Pentagon's commitment.
