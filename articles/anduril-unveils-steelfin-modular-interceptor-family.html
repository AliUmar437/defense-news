Anduril Unveils Steelfin, a Modular Family of Affordable Interceptors for Drone, Cruise and Hypersonic Threats

Michael ReynoldsOct 12, 2026 12:40 UTC 3 min readRegion: North America

WASHINGTON - Anduril Industries has unveiled Steelfin, a family of configurable surface-to-air interceptors designed to defeat cruise missiles, ballistic missiles, hypersonic weapons and even low-cost attack drones - at what the company calls a fraction of the cost of current interceptors.

The company announced Steelfin on Monday at the Association of the U.S. Army's annual meeting in Washington, its first entry into a market long dominated by Lockheed Martin and Raytheon - and the latest move in an industry sprint toward cheaper interceptors built in the thousands rather than the hundreds.

Steelfin is built around a common two-stage architecture. The first stage is a swappable solid rocket booster - Anduril says it is integrating boosters of 10-inch, 18-inch and 21-inch diameters, including quad-pack and dual-pack arrangements for the Navy's Mk 41 Vertical Launch System. The second stage is a shared "terminal interceptor" carrying the same baseline airframe, avionics, software, flight controls and interfaces across the family, with hit-to-kill and blast-fragmentation options planned.

Built to fire from what the military already owns

The main selling point is launcher compatibility: Steelfin variants are designed to fire from the M903A2 launcher used by Patriot batteries, the Navy's Mk 41 VLS, the Army's Typhon mid-range launcher and emerging containerized launchers, working with existing fire control systems like Aegis and the Army's Integrated Battle Command System. Anduril plans to build several thousand missiles a year on shared tooling so output can shift to whichever variant is in demand.

Anduril has not yet flown a Steelfin missile - it has completed ballistic testing, plans flight tests next year and targets full-rate production in late 2028 or early 2029, declining to name the customers it says co-invested in the multi-year effort.

The cost argument

The central claim is cost: executives say one configuration should cost roughly a third of a Patriot PAC-3 MSE interceptor - about $5.3 million per round in the Army's latest budget, versus roughly $15.5 million for a THAAD interceptor and $2.5 million for the Navy's newest SM-2.

"Our military and our allies' militaries are struggling with a set of legacy interceptors that are not scalable, and are not able to match the volume of the threat that they are facing," Chris Brose, Anduril's president and chief strategy officer, told reporters ahead of the announcement.

The timing is deliberate. This month alone, Raytheon received contracts worth up to $6.3 billion for SM-3 Block IB interceptors and up to $24.4 billion for SM-6 missiles, and Lockheed Martin unveiled its PAC-3 Edge interceptor on Friday. The Army has also sought proposals for a Patriot-compatible interceptor below $1 million a round - a requirement Anduril says Steelfin is vying for, alongside the service's Future Interceptor program.

Why it matters: the magazine problem is now a factory problem

The most important thing about Steelfin is not any single performance claim - it is the diagnosis behind the design. Combat in the Middle East and Ukraine has made the interceptor shortage structural: defenders keep spending exquisite, million-dollar missiles against cheap drones and massed salvos, and no existing production line can replace what gets fired. This month's $6.3 billion SM-3 deal and $24.4 billion SM-6 award are the Pentagon's answer so far, and both are multi-year attempts to refill magazines that combat keeps draining.

Steelfin's bet is that the bottleneck is architecture, not just money. Legacy interceptors were built as bespoke weapons, each with its own tooling, suppliers and production line, which is why output cannot surge when demand spikes. A family sharing one terminal stage and one line tries to industrialize the interceptor like a commercial platform - redirecting capacity toward whichever threat is surging.

That mirrors where the Navy itself is headed: its Naval Modular Missile initiative envisions a similar common-terminal-stage family, aiming to eventually replace the Standard Missile family. The caveats are real - Steelfin has not flown, has no named customer, and Anduril has never built a surface-to-air missile. But with Lockheed answering on cost through the PAC-3 Edge and the Pentagon writing multi-year checks to rebuild stockpiles, Anduril has planted a flag on the exact intersection of affordability and volume every service is trying to solve.
