Pentagon Sends 600 Soldiers to Latin America as Anti-Cartel Campaign Expands

David SullivanOct 16, 2026 12:42 UTC 3 min readRegion: Latin America

A 600-soldier battalion from the Army's 10th Mountain Division has begun deploying to Latin America, the latest escalation in the Pentagon's expanding military campaign against drug-trafficking networks in the Western Hemisphere.

The battalion, based at Fort Drum, New York, was first reported by The New York Times on Oct. 14. A senior U.S. official confirmed the deployment to Fox News, saying the troops are being forward-staged under U.S. Southern Command, which will determine where they are ultimately assigned.

The troops will begin at a new jungle warfare facility in Panama, then work with partner forces in Colombia and Honduras. They may also support special operations personnel advising local militaries conducting raids against suspected drug traffickers. Several hundred additional soldiers from the 10th Mountain are expected to help staff a new Western Hemisphere command based in Miami.

SOUTHCOM declined to confirm the movements. "We do not disclose details or comment on U.S. personnel deployment or operational movements due to operational security concerns," media relations officer Steven McLoud told reporters.

An expanding campaign

The deployment is the largest conventional troop movement yet in a campaign that has steadily intensified over the past year. U.S. forces have struck dozens of suspected drug-trafficking boats across Latin America and the Caribbean, killing more than 235 people, according to Fox News. MQ-9 Reaper drones once used to hunt jihadists in Africa have been redeployed to Colombia and Ecuador.

On Oct. 2, 500 troops were sent to the Rio Grande Valley on the U.S.-Mexico border. In September, American special forces partnered with Ecuadorian law enforcement to take down a trafficking ring tied to Mexican cartels.

The campaign's next phase may move from sea to land. Gen. Francis Donovan, the SOUTHCOM commander, told The Wall Street Journal he sees land strikes as an option. "Do I see land strikes as options? Yes. We want all options at the table," he said.

Why it matters

A battalion deployment is a different animal from anything the campaign has used so far. Until now, the operation has run on drones, small special-forces teams and maritime strike assets. Sending a full infantry battalion — trained for combat, not counternarcotics — signals the Pentagon is preparing for a long, hands-on presence rather than a series of one-off strikes.

The Panama jungle facility is the detail worth watching. A dedicated training site suggests permanence: the United States is building infrastructure to rotate troops through the region indefinitely, training alongside Panamanian, Colombian and Honduran forces. That is the classic shape of a security-cooperation mission that grows by inches.

It also raises hard questions. The campaign has already drawn criticism from legal experts over strikes that killed more than 235 suspected traffickers at sea. Moving those operations ashore — with conventional troops advising or supporting raids inside foreign countries — compounds the legal and diplomatic exposure. Partner governments may welcome the help against traffickers, but sustained U.S. ground operations in their territory are a political risk few of them bargained for publicly.

For now, the Pentagon's public posture is deliberate silence. What is known is the direction of travel: more troops, more countries, and — if Donovan's remarks are the roadmap — a campaign that no longer stops at the water's edge.
