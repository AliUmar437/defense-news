Army Lasers Blast Cartel Drones Out of the Sky in Border Test

Michael ReynoldsAug 28, 2026 22:02 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

An Army high-energy laser has shot down three hostile drones near the southern border over two nights in August, in one of the clearest real-world demonstrations yet that directed-energy weapons have moved from the laboratory to the fight.

Service members assigned to Joint Task Force Southern Border — a U.S. Northern Command unit that supports Customs and Border Protection — used the Army's Multi-Purpose High Energy Laser, known as AMP-HEL, to engage and defeat the three drones on Aug. 25 and 26. The aircraft were judged hostile based on their direct support of activity threatening U.S. military personnel and CBP partners, according to the Defense Department, and the footage of the engagements was later released by the Department of War.

Cartels in the crosshairs

The drones were believed to be linked to Mexican drug cartels, which have made small unmanned aircraft a standard part of their smuggling operations. Cartel networks use cheap quadcopters to scout Border Patrol positions, monitor American personnel, and watch for gaps in border defenses — all without putting an operative physically across the border.

"Cartel networks are increasingly employing unmanned aircraft systems to facilitate illicit human-smuggling and actively spy on our personnel and law enforcement partners," said Army Maj. Gen. Curtis Taylor, commander of Joint Task Force Southern Border. "By integrating layered countermeasures alongside advanced directed-energy capabilities like this laser system, we have made it clear that we will not tolerate hostile surveillance."

The AMP-HEL system was built with directed-energy technology from AeroVironment, and its deployment to the border marks a significant operational milestone. High-energy lasers have been "five years away" for decades, dogged by problems with power, cooling, and keeping a beam steady on a moving target long enough to burn through it. The August engagements suggest those problems are now being solved in the field.

A surge of incursions

The laser shots came against a backdrop of escalating drone activity. The military detected 378 drone incursions along the southern border in the previous 30 days alone, Brig. Gen. Brian Filler, the task force's deputy commander for operations, told reporters — and the trend is increasing. Most of the aircraft are commercial off-the-shelf models carrying surveillance cameras, often observed flying alongside cross-border smuggling movements.

And the August engagement was not an isolated event. The U.S. military said earlier in October it had downed 11 cartel drones with high-energy lasers since deploying the technology to the border region in August.

The Army has responded by making the desert a proving ground. At Falcon Peak, a Northern Command exercise held in September at Yuma Proving Ground in Arizona, soldiers watched as defense firms demonstrated radars, interceptor drones, autonomous gun systems, and directed-energy weapons designed to detect, track, and destroy small unmanned aircraft. The exercise established permanent counter-drone testing lanes at Yuma — a sign the Army expects the small-drone threat to keep growing.

Lasers change the cost math

What makes the laser engagements striking is the economics. A cartel drone costs a few hundred or a few thousand dollars. Shooting one down with a missile interceptor costing hundreds of thousands of dollars is a losing trade; dropping one with a beam that costs roughly the price of the fuel running its generator is not. A laser weapon can engage targets repeatedly, at the speed of light, for as long as it has power and a clear line of sight.

That cost exchange is also the military's answer to a lesson from Ukraine, where cheap drones have transformed the battlefield and driven rapid, field-level innovation in both attack and defense. Army planners say they want to reproduce that rapid innovation cycle at home — and the border, with its daily stream of hostile drones, has become their laboratory.

The Army has not said whether the laser will be deployed to the border permanently. But with 11 confirmed kills and hundreds of incursions a month, the case for keeping it there is writing itself.
