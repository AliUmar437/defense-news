Anduril and U.S. Navy Announce $6.6 Billion Push to Expand Submarine Industrial Base

James CarterOct 06, 2026 21:24 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Anduril Industries and the U.S. Navy have unveiled a $6.6 billion plan to expand America's submarine industrial base, anchored by a giant new manufacturing complex at Sparrows Point, Maryland.

The announcement came Oct. 6 at Tradepoint Atlantic, the former Bethlehem Steel site on Baltimore's waterfront, with President Donald Trump and Maryland Gov. Wes Moore on hand. Anduril will invest $3.7 billion of private capital to build Arsenal-2, a software-defined shipyard of more than two million square feet on a 187-acre site. The Navy has awarded the company a contract worth up to $2.9 billion, with payments tied to demonstrated production results rather than paid upfront — a structure the company says puts most of the execution risk on Anduril, not the taxpayer.

Components, not whole submarines

Arsenal-2 will manufacture complex components and large assemblies for the Virginia-class attack submarine — items such as torpedo tubes and hull sections — and deliver them to General Dynamics Electric Boat and HII's Newport News Shipbuilding for final assembly. It will not build complete boats. The idea is to add a parallel source for the hardest-to-make parts, letting more construction proceed at once across the industrial base.

The planned facility includes high-bay assembly halls, fabrication areas, reconfigurable production lines, and a 1,200-foot graving dock, with road, rail, and waterfront connections for moving outsized assemblies. It will run on ArsenalOS, Anduril's manufacturing software, coordinating fabrication, assembly, material movement, inspections, and documentation. Permitting and site preparation are beginning now; initial operations are planned for 2030.

The project is expected to create 3,100 direct jobs and more than 11,000 indirect positions, generating about $2 billion in annual economic output.

Why submarines can't wait

U.S. submarine production has been the Navy's pacing problem for years. The service wants to build two Virginia-class boats a year while simultaneously constructing the Columbia-class ballistic missile submarine — a workload the current base of two nuclear-certified yards and their suppliers has struggled to carry, with skilled labor scarce and lead times stretching.

On top of domestic demand sit the AUKUS commitments: Australia is to receive three, and possibly five, Virginia-class submarines starting in 2032. Meeting that pledge without shortchanging the U.S. fleet requires substantial growth in suppliers, infrastructure, and workforce — exactly the gap Arsenal-2 is meant to fill.

"Submarines are our oceans' apex predators," Vice Adm. Robert Gaucher, who oversees Navy submarine construction, said at the announcement — adding that they are needed "now more than ever" for deterrence, while acknowledging the country's shortfall in shipbuilding capacity.

An unusual deal structure

In a surprise flourish, Trump said American taxpayers would receive a 40 percent stake in the new shipyard — held through a new Anduril subsidiary created to operate the facility, not as equity in Anduril itself. Further details were not immediately available.

"Today, we're officially launching the construction of the largest new shipyard in the United States," Trump said. "Since World War II, there's been nothing like what we're doing today."

For Anduril — a company built on drones, software, and autonomous systems — the move into heavy manufacturing is a leap. The bet is that tech-industry production methods can succeed where traditional shipbuilding has strained. If Arsenal-2 delivers, it could reshape how America builds undersea weapons. If it stumbles, the submarine programs counting on its parts will feel it first.
