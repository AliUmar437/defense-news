USAF's New HACM Hypersonic Cruise Missile Makes First Release Over Australian Range

Robert HayesOct 05, 2026 23:27 UTC 3 min readRegion: Indo-Pacific

The U.S. Air Force has carried out the first flight-test release of its Hypersonic Attack Cruise Missile over an Australian range, moving a long-delayed weapon into the air at last.

The War Zone first reported the test on Oct. 5. An Air Force spokesperson confirmed it the next day, saying the flight took place over "a designated Australian test range" and gathered "crucial data to advance the program's objectives." The spokesperson declined to give a date, describe the launch aircraft, or discuss speed and duration. Open-source reporting widely places the test at the Woomera range in South Australia's desert, the West's favored proving ground for hypersonic flight.

A U.S.-Australian program

The test was conducted under the Southern Cross Integrated Flight Research Experiment, or SCIFiRE — a U.S.-Australian collaboration the spokesperson described as built on more than 15 years of partnership. Australia's vast, empty ranges offer the long flight corridors hypersonic weapons need, and Canberra is a full partner in the weapon: in Australian service it would be the country's first hypersonic missile and its fastest weapon, with planned carriage on the F/A-18F Super Hornet, EA-18G Growler, F-35A, and P-8A.

RTX's Raytheon is the prime contractor for the munition, with Northrop Grumman building the scramjet engine. The Air Force formally established the HACM program in 2022 after a 2020 industry solicitation. Its roots run deeper: the design draws on DARPA's Hypersonic Air-breathing Weapon Concept and, further back, the Boeing X-51 Waverider flights. The weapon has reportedly been designated AHM-4A under a new joint nomenclature series for missiles.

How it works

HACM is an air-breathing hypersonic cruise missile. A conventional rocket booster accelerates it to high speed and altitude, then falls away; a scramjet engine takes over, sustaining flight above Mach 5 — more than 3,300 miles per hour — while the missile maneuvers inside the atmosphere. That combination, extreme speed plus the ability to change course in flight, is what makes hypersonic cruise missiles so difficult for air defenses built to track predictable ballistic arcs.

Unlike larger boost-glide weapons, HACM is sized to be carried by tactical fighters as well as bombers, which would let the Air Force hold defended targets at risk from many more platforms. Exact performance figures remain classified.

Delays and the 2027 target

HACM has suffered significant delays — early plans aimed for flight testing years ago — and the Air Force has taken the unusual step of scaling back the overall test program to protect the schedule. The stated goal now is to be ready for a decision on rapid acquisition in 2027.

That urgency reflects the wider race: China and Russia have fielded hypersonic weapons while the United States has none in operational service. HACM is one of two hypersonic programs the Air Force is pursuing, alongside the larger AGM-183 air-launched rapid response weapon.

The spokesperson would say only that the recent test was "part of our continuous work to advance long-range strike capabilities." For a program that has lived mostly in renderings and budget documents, a real release over a real range is the milestone that counts.
