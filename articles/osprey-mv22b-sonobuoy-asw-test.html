Navy-Marine Osprey Test Opens New Anti-Submarine Chapter

Robert HayesSep 23, 2026 16:29 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

An MV-22B Osprey dropped sonobuoys over the Pacific off San Clemente Island, California, on Aug. 19, 2026, completing the first lot acceptance test of the sensors from a tiltrotor — a milestone the Navy says could reshape how it keeps its undersea surveillance inventory honest.

The test brought together the Air Anti-Submarine Warfare Systems Program Office (PMA-264), the V-22 Joint Program Office (PMA-275), and Marine Operational Test and Evaluation Squadron 1 (VMX-1), flying an aircraft out of Marine Corps Air Station Miramar. It was announced Sept. 22 by PMA-264 and detailed in a Defense Department release the following day.

The mission was not a combat exercise. It was an acceptance run for the Navy's Sonobuoy Quality Assurance Program, which pulls random production samples of sonobuoys and drops them over water to verify reliability and acoustic performance before the lots reach operational squadrons. Until now, that job belonged to the P-3C Orions of Air Test and Evaluation Squadron 30 (VX-30), using automated cockpit-triggered launchers.

Manual drops from the ramp

The Osprey did it the hard way. With the rear cargo ramp open, crew chiefs manually removed the buoys' container caps and inverted the launch tubes into the slipstream — a hands-on evolution a world away from the P-3's push-button dispensers. Defense Department photographs show the loading and rigging work at Miramar in the days before the flight.

Navy Capt. Greg Hinkle, PMA-264's program manager, said the focus of the quality assurance program is making sure the sonobuoys delivered to the fleet perform exactly as expected, and that using the Osprey for the mission reinforces the supply chain and ensures front-line aircrews have mission-ready stores to detect and track undersea threats.

The test gives the Navy a dependable substitute if its primary test platforms are unavailable, preventing backlogs in the acceptance pipeline as the P-3C fleet winds down.

The test crew came from VMX-1, the Marine Corps' operational test squadron at Miramar — the same community that wrings out every new Marine aircraft before it reaches the fleet. Their involvement signals that the Navy views the Osprey not as a one-off test mule but as a standing member of the sonobuoy enterprise.

There is precedent. A 2025 photograph from the Philippine Sea showed a VMM-265 crew chief handling sonobuoys aboard an Osprey with the 31st Marine Expeditionary Unit — evidence that the fleet has been quietly working the problem for at least a year. The August test formalized it: the tiltrotor is now a validated, dependable substitute in the quality-assurance chain, the backstop that keeps acceptance testing moving when the P-3Cs are down or tasked elsewhere.

A door left open

The official release frames the event as laying the groundwork for expanded tactical employment across the fleet — careful language that stops short of declaring the Osprey an operational submarine hunter. But the implications are hard to miss. A tiltrotor that can sprint to a datum far faster than a helicopter and then slow to work a contact has obvious appeal for anti-submarine warfare, and Marine crews have been experimenting at the margins for years: a 2025 photograph showed a VMM-265 crew chief handling sonobuoys in the Philippine Sea.

For now, the verified achievement is narrower and arguably more important: the supply chain that feeds the fleet's ears in the water has a new, faster backup. In a Pacific where submarines are the pacing threat, that is not a small thing.
