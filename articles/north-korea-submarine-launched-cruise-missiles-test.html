North Korea Test-Fires Two Types of Submarine-Launched Strategic Cruise Missiles

Robert HayesOct 15, 2026 12:40 UTC 3 min readRegion: Indo-Pacific

North Korea said Thursday it had test-fired two kinds of submarine-launched strategic cruise missiles the day before, state media reported, with senior generals overseeing launches in the East Sea as American surveillance aircraft flew long orbits over the Korean Peninsula on the same day.

The tests were conducted Wednesday by the North's Missile Administration in waters off the country's east coast, the Korean Central News Agency reported. Full General Kim Jong-sik, a member of the Workers' Party Central Military Commission, and Full General Jang Chang-ha, the head of the Missile Administration, watched the launches alongside Navy engineers responsible for underwater weapon systems.

According to KCNA, the exercise was designed to examine the "mobilized posture" of the North's underwater combat systems, sharpen the Navy's ability to carry out strategic missions, and demonstrate the reliability of its war deterrents. The report did not name the missile types, did not say whether they were fired from an actual submarine or an underwater test platform, and provided no flight data. It added that the test had no adverse impact on the security of neighboring countries.

The South Korean military did not announce the launches, and Seoul had not publicly commented as of Thursday morning. Cruise missiles fly low and slow compared with ballistic missiles, making them harder for long-range radars to detect — one reason North Korean weapons claims are sometimes difficult to verify independently.

Eyes over the peninsula

The tests unfolded under watchful eyes. A U.S. Navy MQ-4C Triton, a high-altitude, long-endurance maritime surveillance drone, flew over the central Korean Peninsula and the East Sea for about nine hours on Wednesday, according to a Voice of America report citing Flightradar24 tracking data and carried by the Korea JoongAng Daily. The drone left Okinawa, Japan, around 2 a.m., entered South Korean airspace near Ansan and traced roughly ten passes back and forth along the boundary of South Korea's northern airspace before departing around 1 p.m.

A U.S. Army ARES reconnaissance and electronic-warfare aircraft was also airborne overnight, flying between the East Sea and the Yellow Sea after departing the Pyeongtaek area just before midnight. The ARES is built to collect electronic intelligence — communications and radar signals — from a modified Bombardier business jet.

A busy month of testing

The cruise-missile test was North Korea's fourth launch in a month. On Monday, the North test-fired what state media called a tactical ballistic missile, describing it as a successful technical upgrade of an important core element without elaborating. The tests came one day after President Donald Trump said he may meet with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un during an upcoming visit to Asia.

North Korea has been building an undersea deterrent for years. In January 2024 it test-fired two Pulhwasal-3-31 ("Fire Arrow-3-31") submarine-launched strategic cruise missiles off the eastern port of Sinpo, with Kim Jong Un declaring the "nuclear weaponization of the navy" an urgent task.

Why it matters

Two details stand out. First, KCNA framed this test not as a range milestone but as a readiness exercise — "mobilized posture" and operating skills — suggesting Pyongyang is less interested in advertising a new missile than in proving its crews and systems can launch on demand. A submarine-launched cruise missile is only a deterrent if the submarine, the crew and the command chain can be trusted to work together.

Second, the timing looks deliberate. The test lands in the news cycle right as Trump floats a meeting with Kim — a familiar North Korean posture of demonstrating capability before any talks begin. With diplomacy between Washington and Pyongyang frozen, the North's fourth test in a month reads as a signal that it will not let the conversation move on without it.

The observable part of this story is the surveillance. Even as Pyongyang withheld the technical details, the Triton and ARES flights show the United States is collecting on every test — data that feeds American and South Korean assessments regardless of what KCNA chooses to publish. What to watch next: whether KCNA names the missiles or releases imagery, as it has for prior tests, and whether the pace of testing holds through Trump's Asia trip.
