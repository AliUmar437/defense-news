Secretive AIM-260 Missile Spotted on Australian Super Hornet

James CarterOct 01, 2026 17:01 UTC 3 min readRegion: Indo-Pacific

A U.S. Navy photograph released Sept. 30, 2026, shows something airpower watchers have waited years to see clearly: an inert AIM-260 Joint Advanced Tactical Missile riding the cheek station of an Australian F/A-18F Super Hornet.

The image, taken Sept. 11 by Navy photographer Dana Rene White, captures the Royal Australian Air Force jet — assigned to No. 1 Squadron — taking off from Naval Base Ventura County Point Mugu in California during Gray Flag 2026, the Navy's sprawling test exercise. The missile's blue bands mark it as an inert round: no live rocket motor, no warhead.

The loadout tells its own story. Alongside the AIM-260, the jet carried inert CATM-9X and CATM-120 training rounds, three external fuel tanks, an AN/ASQ-228 ATFLIR targeting pod, and a Tactical Combat Training System pod — a test configuration, not a war load.

Australia's $480 million bet

The sighting came weeks after Australia announced in August 2026 that it would buy the AIM-260 through U.S. Foreign Military Sales, becoming the first country outside the United States to operate the missile. The deal is valued at roughly $480 million — reported in Australia as almost 736 million Australian dollars — with RAAF Super Hornets first in line, followed by the F-35A and the EA-18G Growler. The MQ-28 Ghost Bat has been named as a possible future candidate.

Canberra's own language was measured: the acquisition, Australia said, will give its air force a credible deterrent against airborne targets, complementing existing air-to-air missile capabilities. No delivery timeline has been disclosed.

The missile meant to outrange the threat

The AIM-260, built by Lockheed Martin under a program the Pentagon launched in 2017, is designed to outrange the AIM-120 AMRAAM it will eventually succeed — prior reporting puts its reach at 120 miles or more — while keeping a similar form factor so it fits the internal weapons bays of the F-22 and F-35. Its development has been paced by China's PL-15, the very-long-range missile carried by the J-20.

The program is accelerating. In September 2026 the Pentagon and Lockheed Martin signed a framework agreement to push production and multiyear procurement, and the Air Force Test Center has confirmed both ground and airborne testing. The first-ever public photo of a U.S. AIM-260 appeared only in May 2026, on a VX-31 test jet at Eglin Air Force Base in Florida — making the Australian sighting the second public glimpse, and the first involving a foreign operator.

Gray Flag itself was the largest to date: more than 3,000 personnel, 150 organizations, 750 sorties, and 2,000 flight hours flown from the Point Mugu Sea Range.

The missile sighting is part of a wider U.S.-Australian air-weapons partnership. RAAF Super Hornets are also involved in the Hypersonic Attack Cruise Missile effort under the Southern Cross Integrated Flight Research Experiment, or SCIFiRE — a separate program, but evidence of how deeply the two air forces have intertwined their long-range strike planning.

One caution from the analysts who first published the photo: it does not establish whether the missile was Australian-owned, a U.S. test asset, or part of a specific integration effort. What it does establish is that the West's next long-range air-to-air missile is now flying on allied jets — inert for now, in public for the first time.
