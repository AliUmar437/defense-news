Pentagon, Lockheed Weigh Multi-Year F-35 Production and Sustainment Deal

Robert HayesOct 09, 2026 10:18 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

The Pentagon and Lockheed Martin are discussing a sweeping multi-year agreement for the F-35 that could span up to a decade and cover both building the jets and keeping them flying — a deal potentially worth tens of billions of dollars.

The talks were first reported by Breaking Defense on Oct. 9. The idea is not new: officials have weighed multi-year F-35 procurement for years. But the current push, driven by the administration's enthusiasm for long-term weapons agreements, would go further than past efforts by bundling production with sustainment — the maintenance, spares, and support that have long been the program's costliest headache.

What a decade-long deal would include

"There's been talk about a 10-year, multi-year buy that would include the production as well as sustainment," Matt Milas, president of Honeywell Aerospace's defense division, told Breaking Defense in September. Honeywell is a major F-35 supplier.

A central element would be bulk purchases of spare parts, with the increased flow of components expected to begin around the jet's 21st production lot, roughly three years out. Flooding the supply chain with spares is seen as the most direct lever for lifting the fleet's readiness rates, which have long trailed the Pentagon's targets.

The skeptics

Not everyone is convinced the structure fits. A person familiar with the negotiations, speaking on condition of anonymity, confirmed the talks but questioned their premise: multi-year procurement "makes sense where the department has seen success with munitions contracts. It makes less sense for aircraft lot buys" — particularly while elements of F-35 modernization are still in development. "The complexities of sustainment present multiple unique challenges," the person added, doubting Lockheed could demonstrate genuine long-term savings.

The skepticism has history behind it. The F-35's sustainment bill — the cost of operating and maintaining the fleet over its lifetime — has been the program's most stubborn problem for a decade, with per-flight-hour costs resisting years of reduction campaigns. With more than 1,000 jets delivered worldwide, even small shifts in sustainment costs move tens of billions of dollars.

The backdrop

The talks come days after the Pentagon finalized its deal for F-35 Lots 18 and 19: 296 aircraft for $24.3 billion, an average of $82.4 million per airframe, with deliveries starting this year. Lot 20 is expected to be negotiated as a multi-year contract now that the program is in full-rate production, and Lockheed chief executive Jim Taiclet has said the company can sustain production at 156 jets a year on the strength of U.S. and allied demand.

The Pentagon also continues to spend heavily on the support tail: a recent $1.6 billion spare-parts award was described as the largest in the program's history, and the Navy added $871 million to Lockheed's site-activation contract for standing up new F-35 operating locations.

No timeline for an agreement has been announced. Whether the two sides can bridge the gap between the Pentagon's appetite for long-term savings and industry's caution about locking in sustainment costs will determine whether the decade-long deal becomes the template for the fighter's future — or another idea that stalls in negotiation.
