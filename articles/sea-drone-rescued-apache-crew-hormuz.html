Sea Drone That Rescued Downed Apache Crew Hailed by Pentagon Chief

James CarterSep 18, 2026 22:05 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Secretary of War Pete Hegseth stood on the factory floor of Saronic Technologies in Austin, Texas, on Sept. 18, 2026, and called the startup "the gold standard" of American military manufacturing. He had a story to tell about why.

Three months earlier, on June 8, a U.S. Army AH-64 Apache patrolling over the Strait of Hormuz went down off the coast of Oman after taking Iranian fire — President Donald Trump later confirmed that Iran had shot the helicopter down. The two crew members ended up in the water, roughly two hours from help, in airspace too dangerous for a manned rescue helicopter.

The Navy sent a boat with no crew at all.

The vessel was a Corsair, a 24-foot autonomous surface vessel built by Saronic and operated by the 5th Fleet's Task Force 59, the Navy's unmanned-systems experimentation unit. It reached the downed aviators, who hauled themselves aboard, then moved them to a second location because of what U.S. Central Command described as operational circumstances. From there a helicopter hoisted them out. Both crew members were reported stable.

Defense officials believe it was the first known personnel recovery ever carried out by an unmanned surface vessel.

The "Arsenal of Freedom" tour

Hegseth's Austin stop was part of a swing he branded the "Arsenal of Freedom" tour, aimed at showcasing the commercial shipyards and startups the Pentagon wants to pull into its industrial base. Saronic's chief executive, Dino Mavrookas, introduced him, and the secretary used the visit to promise fast contracts for companies that deliver — and the door for those that don't.

The Corsair that made the pickup is no prototype. The 24-foot boat tops 35 knots, ranges 1,000 nautical miles, and carries a 1,000-pound payload. Saronic says it went from prototype to serial production of 300 hulls in under a year, backed by a $392 million late-2025 production contract and a $1.75 billion funding round in March 2026. The company is also building "Port Alpha," a $3.2 billion shipyard in South Texas whose first phase is expected to be operational by 2028.

Why a robot boat

The rescue's backstory explains the decision. The Apache was one of at least seven crewed U.S. aircraft lost to Iranian fire since February 2026, during the U.S.-Iran conflict that began with Operation Epic Fury. Sending a manned helicopter into the same airspace to hover over survivors was judged too risky; the unmanned boat could go where crews could not.

CENTCOM said the operation was run by Naval Forces Central Command with the 82nd Airborne Division, and spokesman Capt. Tim Hawkins confirmed the Corsair's role to DefenseScoop. Saronic co-founder Rob Lehman later walked through the mission on a DefenseScoop podcast, describing a vessel designed for exactly this kind of dull, dangerous work.

The Pentagon has not said whether the Corsair's rescue will become doctrine. But on a factory floor in Austin, the secretary of war treated it as a preview — of a fleet where the first responder to a downed crew might not have a crew at all.
