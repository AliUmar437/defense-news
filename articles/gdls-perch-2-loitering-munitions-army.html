GDLS to Deliver New PERCH 2.0 Loitering Munition Prototypes to Army This Month

James CarterOct 09, 2026 16:35 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

General Dynamics Land Systems will deliver four prototypes of its PERCH 2.0 loitering munition system to the Army by the end of October, putting a new tank-launched drone capability into soldiers' hands within weeks.

The prototypes are headed to the 1st Brigade, 1st Cavalry Division at Fort Hood, where troops will integrate them onto Abrams tanks for experimentation, Scott Taylor, GDLS's director of U.S. growth, told Breaking Defense. The upgraded system is set for its formal debut at the AUSA exposition in Washington this month.

What PERCH is

PERCH — Precision Effects & Reconnaissance, Canister-Housed — is a modular kit that bolts onto M1A2 Abrams SEPv3 tanks and Stryker vehicles and lets crews launch AeroVironment Switchblade 300 loitering munitions without opening their hatches. The munitions serve as both scouts and strike weapons: loitering over the battlefield to find targets beyond the line of sight, then diving onto them.

The 2.0 iteration reflects direct Army feedback on the first version, which GDLS debuted at last year's AUSA. Soldiers asked for better beyond-line-of-sight capability, easier reloads, and more onboard storage — addressed in part by switching from the larger Switchblade 600 to the smaller Switchblade 300 — plus the ability to fire while fully buttoned up, preserving the vehicle's armor and chemical-biological protection.

The system was refined after a December 2025 demonstration at the Machine Assisted Rugged Sapper event at Fort Hood, where the launcher was tested on Abrams tanks in realistic mission scenarios.

Built for Ukraine's lessons

GDLS developed the original PERCH with its own research funding, Taylor said, in direct response to the war in Ukraine, feedback from U.S. commanders in Europe, and discussions with III Armored Corps leadership. Loitering munitions have become one of the war's defining weapons, used by the thousands against armor, artillery, and air defenses.

The goal is to give armored brigades their own organic drone-strike capability — the ability to see and hit targets beyond ridgelines and buildings — rather than waiting on assets controlled at higher echelons. In GDLS's concept, the tank becomes a node in a sensor-to-shooter network, launching expendable eyes that can turn into precision weapons when a target appears.

An urgent Army market

The Army has been moving quickly to push one-way attack drones down to brigade level and below, and several companies are competing for a role. GDLS's advantage is incumbency: it builds the Abrams and the Stryker, and PERCH is designed as a bolt-on kit requiring no permanent changes to the vehicle — a replacement of the turret's rear sponson box and mounting on existing hardpoints.

The company has not disclosed the system's range, warhead details, or expected unit cost. What happens at Fort Hood over the coming months — how soldiers actually use the prototypes, what breaks, what gets requested — will shape whether PERCH 2.0 moves from experiment to a program of record. For now, the prototypes are built, the brigade is waiting, and the clock is ticking toward the end-of-October delivery.

The munition at the heart of the system, AeroVironment's Switchblade 300, is a tube-launched loitering weapon already in U.S. service, small enough to be carried in numbers and precise enough for targets that do not justify larger fires. Pairing it with armor gives tank crews something they have never organically had: their own aerial reconnaissance that can become a strike asset in seconds, without calling up the chain of command.
