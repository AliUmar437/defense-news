Australia Receives Second HIMARS Tranche Two Years Ahead of Schedule

Michael ReynoldsOct 09, 2026 12:43 UTC 3 min readRegion: Indo-Pacific

Australia has taken delivery of a second tranche of U.S.-built HIMARS rocket launchers — two years ahead of schedule and just a year after the first.

The announcement came Oct. 7 from the Defence Minister. The launchers go to the Army's long-range fires regiment in Adelaide, part of the 10th Fires Brigade, significantly expanding a capability Canberra considers central to its military reorientation toward the Indo-Pacific.

Ahead of schedule, twice over

Early delivery has become something of a theme for Australia's strike programs. The country fired a Precision Strike Missile from a HIMARS during Exercise Talisman Sabre 2025 — two years ahead of schedule — and earlier this year successfully test-fired the first Australian-manufactured Guided Multiple Launch Rocket System missile from one of its own launchers.

"These highly mobile systems are an incredibly important platform and the delivery of these additional units represents a significant leap in capability for the Australian Army," Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles said.

The 90-launcher plan

The second tranche feeds a much larger ambition. In April, the government announced additional investment in HIMARS and Precision Strike Missiles to stand up a second long-range fires regiment. At full strength, Australia plans to operate up to 90 HIMARS across two regiments within the 10th Fires Brigade.

The ranges are growing too. PrSM gives the force the ability to strike targets up to 500 kilometers away; future increments are expected to push beyond 1,000 kilometers — a reach that would let land-based launchers hold distant maritime and land targets at risk across Australia's northern approaches.

Building missiles at home

Canberra is pairing the imported launchers with a sovereign munitions effort. Australia and the United States established a cooperative PrSM program last year, and missile production is already underway at a new facility in South Australia, with a second factory due to open near Newcastle, New South Wales, next year.

The investments sit inside the 2026 National Defence Strategy and Integrated Investment Program: up to $37 billion for long-range strike capabilities, and up to $36 billion over the decade for the Guided Weapons and Explosive Ordnance Enterprise.

Why HIMARS

The truck-mounted system earned its reputation in Ukraine, where precision rockets destroyed Russian ammunition depots and command posts far behind the front lines. For Australia, the appeal is the same: mobile, hard-to-find launchers able to deliver precise long-range fires — and, increasingly, Australian-made rockets to feed them.

The first Australian HIMARS were displayed publicly at the Avalon airshow in 2025. With the second tranche now in country two years early, the program has become a rare bright spot in a procurement landscape more often defined by delay — and a signal of the priority Washington places on arming allies in the region.

The program's pace stands out against its own original plan. The first tranche began with two launchers shown publicly at the Avalon airshow in 2025, and the delivery schedule at the time called for six more by mid-April, another 12 during 2026, and the remaining 22 before the end of 2028. The second tranche arriving two years early suggests both production and the U.S.-Australian coordination behind it are running well ahead of expectations.
