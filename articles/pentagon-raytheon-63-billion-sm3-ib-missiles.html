Pentagon, Raytheon Sign Up to $6.3 Billion Multi-Year Deal for SM-3 IB Missiles

James CarterOct 08, 2026 22:07 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

Raytheon has won a multi-year Pentagon contract worth up to $6.3 billion to build and sustain Standard Missile-3 Block IB interceptors, the Navy's principal defense against short- and intermediate-range ballistic missiles.

The Missile Defense Agency awarded the deal on Oct. 7, and Raytheon announced it the following day. Listed in the Pentagon's daily awards digest under contract number HQ0851-27-C-E001, it runs for five years with options for two more. The base value is $4.43 billion; if the options are exercised, the ceiling rises to about $6.34 billion.

The award is structured as an undefinitized contract action, a mechanism that lets work begin immediately while final terms are negotiated. The government obligated $454.2 million in fiscal 2026 procurement funds at signing — a fraction of the ceiling, and not the amount Raytheon will ultimately be paid. Work is centered on Raytheon's missile facilities in Tucson, Arizona, and Huntsville, Alabama, and is expected to run through Oct. 31, 2034.

Replenishing combat-tested stocks

The contract's purpose is straightforward: rebuild the interceptor inventories the United States has spent in real fighting. The SM-3 was used in combat for the first time in 2024, fired by Navy destroyers defending Israel against Iranian ballistic missile barrages. Those engagements proved the weapon — and emptied magazines the Pentagon is now racing to refill.

"This award underscores Raytheon's commitment to increasing production of critical munitions," Raytheon President Phil Jasper said, describing the Block IB as combat-proven and central to keeping fleets and allies defended.

Under Secretary of War for Acquisition and Sustainment Michael P. Duffey said the missile had "proven its operational superiority against any adversary," adding that the contract "provides production certainty to our defense industrial base, breaks sub-tier manufacturing bottlenecks, and ensures that our fleets and combatant commands have the interceptors required."

What the missile does

The SM-3 Block IB is a midcourse interceptor. Launched from Aegis-equipped destroyers and cruisers — or from the Aegis Ashore sites in Europe — it climbs above the atmosphere, releases a kinetic kill vehicle, and destroys incoming ballistic missiles by sheer collision, carrying no explosive warhead of its own. The Block IB added an improved infrared seeker and better steering than earlier variants, sharpening its ability to pick real warheads out from decoys.

Why multi-year matters

Demand for missile defense has climbed on nearly every front. Beyond the Middle East engagements, the war in Ukraine has shown what sustained missile and drone campaigns do to air-defense stocks, and U.S. planners are blunt that American magazines were sized for shorter wars. Multi-year contracts are the Pentagon's preferred answer: steady, predictable orders that let industry invest in tooling and suppliers with confidence rather than surging and stalling on single-year buys.

A munitions buying spree

The award is the latest in a run of outsized Pentagon munitions deals. In October, Raytheon secured a contract worth up to $24.4 billion for Standard Missile-6 interceptors; in July, Lockheed Martin won a $58.6 billion Patriot missile contract. Together they reflect the administration's push to expand weapons production and lock contractors into long-term manufacturing commitments.

Raytheon says it has already been investing in expanded Standard Missile capacity. But industry executives have warned that congressional funding remains the binding constraint: without appropriated dollars, even signed contract ceilings cannot become production lines. For now, the SM-3 line in Tucson has its orders — and the Navy has one less magazine to ration.
