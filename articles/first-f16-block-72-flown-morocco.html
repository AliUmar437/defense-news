First F-16 Block 72 Takes Flight, Seven Years After Morocco's Order

Robert HayesOct 06, 2026 17:09 UTC 3 min readRegion: Global

The first F-16 Block 72 has taken to the air, seven years after Morocco became the launch customer for the newest variant of the Fighting Falcon.

Lockheed Martin announced Oct. 6 that the first jet built for the Royal Moroccan Air Force — a two-seat F-16D — completed its maiden flight at the company's facility in Greenville, South Carolina. The aircraft will now proceed through flight testing ahead of its delivery to Morocco. It is the first Block 72 airframe ever built, making Morocco the variant's launch operator.

Morocco was the first customer to select the Block 72 configuration when the U.S. government approved the proposed sale in March 2019. At the time, the Defense Security Cooperation Agency said Morocco was seeking 25 F-16C/D Block 72 aircraft in a package estimated at $3.787 billion, including 29 Pratt & Whitney F100-PW-229 engines — four of them spares — and 26 AN/APG-83 Scalable Agile Beam Radar (SABR) AESA radars, one of them a spare. The gap between that approval and the maiden flight runs to seven and a half years.

Photos of the flight showed the pilot wearing a Digital Joint Helmet Mounted Cueing System, an advanced helmet not often seen on F-16 pilots, though it is unclear whether Morocco has ordered it.

A Block 70 with a Pratt & Whitney heart

The distinction between the Block 70 and the Block 72 is the engine. The Block 70 uses General Electric's F110-GE-129; the Block 72 uses Pratt & Whitney's F100-PW-229, which Lockheed Martin notes provides fractionally more power. The two configurations otherwise share the same major modernization package: the APG-83 AESA radar, new mission computers, Link 16 data links, modernized navigation, helmet-mounted cueing, and a structural life extended to 12,000 hours.

The F-16 began life as a Pratt & Whitney-powered fighter, and reliability problems with early F100s prompted the Air Force's "Alternate Fighter Engine" competition of the 1980s — the "Great Engine War" that established the F110 as an alternative. U.S. procurement eventually concentrated on GE-powered F-16s, but Pratt & Whitney continued supplying F100-powered jets to international customers.

For Morocco, the choice is about commonality. The Royal Moroccan Air Force already operates F-16C/D Block 52+ aircraft powered by F100 engines, so mechanics, spares, and engine shops carry over to the new fleet. Morocco is also upgrading its 23 remaining Block 52+ aircraft to the F-16V configuration — broadly similar to the new-build Block 70/72 standard — which would grow its F-16 fleet from 23 to 48 aircraft.

Building an industrial base

Lockheed Martin is tying the program to a broader effort to develop Morocco's sustainment infrastructure. The company says it is working with Moroccan industry, including Maintenance Aero Maroc, on a maintenance, repair and overhaul facility at Benslimane spanning more than 8,000 square meters. Initially intended for heavy maintenance of C-130 Hercules aircraft, the facility is planned to expand to additional platforms including the F-16 — a step toward Morocco becoming a regional MRO hub.

Lockheed Martin's partnership with Morocco spans more than 50 years, beginning with the first C-130H delivery in 1974, the company said. Today the Royal Moroccan Armed Forces also operate Black Hawks and advanced radars, and are set to receive HIMARS and AH-64E Apache helicopters.

The new-build Moroccan jets arrive at an interesting point in the F-16 program. The Block 70/72 has attracted customers across Europe, the Middle East, and Asia — Bahrain, Bulgaria, Jordan, Slovakia, Taiwan, Turkey, and others have selected the latest-generation Viper — and the Greenville line now carries a backlog stretching years into the future. Morocco's position as the Block 72 launch customer means the jet now flying is effectively the prototype-standard production aircraft for a configuration other countries can now buy.
