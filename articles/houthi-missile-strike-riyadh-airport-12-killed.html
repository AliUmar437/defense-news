Houthi Missile Strike on Riyadh Airport Kills 12, Wounds 309

James CarterOct 11, 2026 12:41 UTC 4 min readRegion: Global

A missile struck a passenger terminal at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport on Saturday, killing at least 12 people and wounding 309 in Saudi Arabia's deadliest attack in more than a decade, the kingdom's civil aviation authority said.

The strike landed late Saturday afternoon — the third missile attack this week on the airport, which serves a capital of about eight million people. The dead included four Saudis, two Bangladeshis and one national each of the United States, Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Palestine and Sudan, the authority said. The wounded, some in critical condition, were rushed to hospitals by ambulance and helicopter.

More than 600 flights were cancelled and the kingdom's second-busiest airport was closed until at least late Sunday. Schools across Riyadh moved to remote learning for a week. Video from inside the terminal showed debris strewn across a dust-filled hall.

A senior Houthi official, Nasr al-Din Amer, appeared to acknowledge responsibility, writing on X: "We shut down Riyadh international airport." The group later warned airlines and travelers to avoid Saudi airports and airspace — except those serving Mecca and Medina — saying Saudi Arabia "will bear responsibility for any failed attempts to intercept" its missiles.

A week of attacks

Two earlier strikes on the airport this week killed four people and heavily damaged a Saudi commercial aircraft. A Thursday attack destroyed a passenger jet on the tarmac and killed three people, including a pilot, prompting Britain to advise against all but essential travel to Riyadh. Several airlines, including Lufthansa and Emirates, cancelled flights to the capital, and the United States, Britain, Spain and Canada warned their citizens against using the airport.

The Saudi-led coalition's spokesman, Turki al-Malki, called Saturday's attack a war crime against a civilian facility and said the joint forces command would respond firmly within the bounds of international law. The UN's special envoy for Yemen, Hans Grundberg, called the attacks a grave escalation, and the secretary-general condemned strikes on civilian infrastructure as violations of international law.

Trump weighs joining Saudi strikes

President Donald Trump said he was considering joining Saudi strikes against the Houthis in Yemen — a step he had previously resisted. "We may. We're going to look at it," he told reporters, adding that he planned to speak with Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. Last month, Trump rejected two Saudi appeals for direct American strikes, CNN reported at the time.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirmed an American citizen was among the dead and condemned the attack as showing a blatant disregard for innocent civilian life.

The Houthi campaign is the sharp end of a Yemen war that flared up again this summer after years of relative calm. The group began striking Saudi infrastructure, including oil refineries, after announcing in July it would target Saudi shipping in the Red Sea in retaliation for a Saudi blockade on the capital Sanaa. Last month, Houthi forces captured the port of Mocha and closed in on Taiz, Yemen's third-largest city, while a Saudi-backed government offensive struggled to retake ground.

Why it matters

The Houthis have crossed a line they have spent years probing. They have not merely struck Saudi Arabia; they have shown they can repeatedly hit a prestige civilian target in the capital, forcing the closure of an international airport and driving airlines away. That strikes directly at the kingdom's drive to become a global business and tourism destination.

For the air-defense picture, the repeated terminal hits matter as much as the casualties. Saudi Arabia operates some of the world's densest missile-defense coverage, yet missiles are getting through, reportedly as the kingdom's interceptor stocks run low — the AP reported last month that Riyadh had sought air-defense help from France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt. It is the same demand signal driving the Pentagon's own rush to replenish stocks and field next-generation interceptors.

And Washington now faces its hardest version of the Houthi question. An American is dead, a partner's capital airport is closed, and the crown prince wants American firepower. Trump resisted twice; a third ask, after this, is much harder to refuse — and American strikes would widen a war already spreading from Yemen's Red Sea coast to Riyadh's departure gates.
