'Red Tails' Train to Keep F-35s Flying Under Fire

Robert HayesAug 17, 2026 16:56 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

For three days in mid-August, Dannelly Field in Montgomery, Alabama, became a war zone — simulated, but only just.

Operation Tuskegee Thunder, held Aug. 12-14, 2026, put the Alabama Air National Guard's 187th Fighter Wing through its paces in a scenario built around a Pacific operating environment. Airmen generated F-35A combat sorties while absorbing simulated attacks, degraded communications, chemical threats, drone incursions, casualties, gate runners, and mass-casualty drills — falling back on alternate and contingency command-and-control methods when their primary networks went down.

The exercise ran in conjunction with the wing's Combat Readiness Inspection, the formal evaluation of whether the Red Tails can generate and sustain combat airpower as part of the total force. For a wing that received its first F-35As in December 2023, transitioning from the F-16, it was the most important milestone of the conversion to date.

"The entire wing has to be part of the fight"

"The F-35 is the most capable fighter aircraft in the world, but readiness is ultimately measured by our airmen's ability to fight and sustain operations in the most challenging conditions," said Col. John D. Caldwell, the wing's commander. "Operation Tuskegee Thunder provided our airmen the opportunity to hone their skills, refine tactics, techniques and procedures, and develop the confidence to operate anywhere, at any time."

Lt. Col. Martin "Lefty" Smith, an F-35 pilot who leads the wing's plans and programs, put the challenge in practical terms: operating a fifth-generation fighter in a contested environment requires the entire wing to be part of the fight — maintaining command and control, protecting and sustaining the force, adapting when the environment changes, and continuing to generate combat airpower. The challenge, he said, isn't simply launching aircraft.

The inspection that matters

A Combat Readiness Inspection is the Air Force's periodic deep audit of a wing's ability to do its wartime job — not just fly, but deploy, communicate, protect, and sustain under pressure. For the 187th, passing through it with the F-35 validates years of conversion work: large-force exercises, night operations, and distributed training since the first Lightnings arrived at Dannelly Field.

The August inspection tested whether all of it holds together when the radios go down and the casualties start coming in — the unglamorous half of airpower, and the half that decides wars. The official releases describe what was tested, not how the wing scored; no outcome has been published.

The wing's path to the Lightning was a long one. The 187th flew the F-16 for decades before the first F-35As touched down at Dannelly Field in December 2023. Since then the conversion has run through the standard syllabus of a fighter wing changing airframes: large-force exercises to learn to fight as a package, night operations, and distributed training away from home station. Tuskegee Thunder was the exam that asked whether the wing could do all of it while someone was shooting back — at least on paper.

A name with history

The Red Tails name honors the Tuskegee Airmen of World War II, the Black fighter pilots whose escort missions over Europe became legend. The 187th has carried that lineage through the F-16 era and into the F-35, and the exercise's name was no accident: Tuskegee Thunder was as much about heritage as readiness.
