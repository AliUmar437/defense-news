Navy Orders 30 Drone Ships in Big Push for Medium Uncrewed Vessels

Michael ReynoldsSep 29, 2026 22:14 UTC 3 min readRegion: United States

The U.S. Navy has ordered 30 medium uncrewed surface vessels from three Louisiana shipbuilders, the service's biggest commitment yet to putting drone ships to sea in numbers.

The announcement came Sept. 29 under the Medium Unmanned Surface Vessel Family of Systems program. Galliano Marine Services, Huntington Ingalls Industries, and Saronic Technologies each received production agreements for 10 vessels, using Other Transaction Authority — a contracting route built for speed rather than the Pentagon's traditional years-long process.

The money and the timeline

Each agreement covers 10 vessels at an average procurement cost of $40 million per hull, which works out to roughly $1.2 billion for the 30-ship run. Production begins immediately at yards in Loreauville, Houma, and Franklin, Louisiana, with the first deliveries due before the end of fiscal year 2027.

Seven companies competed after completing Phase I at-sea testing. The losers — Sea Machines, Leidos, PacMar Technologies, and Birdon — did not get production deals, though three of them received $15 million each for completing the trials and will be listed on the Defense Department's drone marketplace, keeping them eligible for future work.

What the Navy asked for

The MUSV marketplace solicitation, issued in March to replace the earlier Modular Attack Surface Craft effort, called for vessels able to cover 2,500 nautical miles at 25 knots while carrying 25 tons on the payload deck. In Phase I, the Navy tested whether the contenders could sense their maritime surroundings, sail without operator assistance, and stay deployed for extended periods.

Saronic will build its 180-foot Marauder design; HII offered Romulus. Galliano Marine Services, which does business as Edison Chouest, has not publicly detailed its entrant.

"Each agreement is for the procurement of 10 MUSV at an average procurement cost of $40 million per vessel," Chris Miller, the Navy's acting portfolio manager for robotic and autonomous systems, told reporters. He called the awards "a significant step forward in translating the strategic vision into a tangible fleet capability."

Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao framed the move as the service's priority to modernize the fleet quickly with autonomous systems.

Why "medium" matters

Medium USVs sit in the sweet spot the Navy has chased for a decade: big enough to carry meaningful sensors and weapons across ocean distances, small and cheap enough to buy in numbers and risk in contested waters. They are central to the "hybrid fleet" vision — a relatively small number of large manned warships multiplied by scores of robotic ones doing surveillance, mine warfare, electronic warfare, and strike.

Past efforts foundered on reliability, autonomy shortfalls, and shifting requirements. The Navy's plan now is to grow the uncrewed surface fleet from four hulls to 30 by 2030, focused on the Indo-Pacific. Whether these 30 boats become an operational capability or another experiment with hull numbers will depend on what the at-sea testing actually proved — results the Navy has not published in detail.

The choice of Other Transaction Authority is itself a statement of intent. OTA agreements let the Pentagon prototype and buy without the years-long Federal Acquisition Regulation process, and the Navy has used them deliberately here to compress the timeline from competition to hulls in the water. The losing bidders' exclusion from standard protest rights under OTA is part of that trade-off: speed in exchange for the usual legal recourse, a bargain the Navy has decided is worth making as it races to field uncrewed mass in the Pacific.
