Ukraine's F-16 Fleet Faces Readiness Crisis as War Grinds On

Michael ReynoldsSep 30, 2026 17:10 UTC 3 min readRegion: Europe

More than half of Ukraine's donated F-16 fighters are stuck in what a Western official calls "maintenance limbo," and at the current pace Kyiv could exhaust its remaining available aircraft by the end of 2026, the Wall Street Journal reported on Sept. 30, 2026.

The Ukrainian Air Force declined to give the Journal fleet-status figures, so the assessment rests on Western sources — but Reuters independently confirmed the core of it on Oct. 8, reporting that many of the donated jets are back in western Europe for repairs, grounded by a lack of engine parts.

The maintenance bottleneck

Ukraine has received an estimated 40 to 50 F-16s, with some airframes held back in Europe for training and five confirmed lost in combat. Another 110 have been pledged by Belgium, Denmark, the Netherlands, and Norway. The problem is not just airframes — it is the pipeline that keeps them flying.

Long-term maintenance is done in Belgium by Sabena Aerospace Engineering, where phase inspections are taking up to 40 weeks — against two to six weeks in the United States. For a fleet flying daily combat air-defense sorties, that math is brutal.

The timing could hardly be worse. Ukraine's F-16s have become workhorses of the air-defense campaign, thrown against Russian cruise missiles and, increasingly, Russia's new jet-powered Geran-4 and Geran-5 drones. Analyst Michael Kofman has noted the Vipers playing an important role in those intercepts — the kind of grinding, unglamorous work that eats airframes.

Running low on missiles

The jets are also running out of things to shoot. AIM-9 Sidewinders and AIM-120 AMRAAMs have been expended in large numbers against incoming missiles and drones, and the same AMRAAMs feed Ukraine's NASAMS ground-based air-defense batteries — two hungers drawing from one stockpile.

Ukrainian pilots are rationing missile shots and falling back on the F-16's internal 20mm M61A1 Vulcan cannon, with Kyiv negotiating for tens of thousands of additional 20mm rounds. It is a measure of the strain: a fourth-generation fighter reduced to gun runs because the missile pipeline cannot keep up.

The sustainment problem starts long before the flight line. Ukrainian pilots and maintainers trained in the United States, Denmark, and Romania on compressed timelines, and the maintenance culture they entered is unforgiving: an F-16 is a maintenance-hungry aircraft even in peacetime, a demand that only grows under combat tempo and dispersed basing. Spare engines, avionics boxes, and structural parts were always the long poles of the fleet — and they are now the poles that are breaking.

A fleet pressed into a different war

The donated Vipers are older European mid-life-update airframes, pledged for air combat and strike missions. Instead they have been pressed into the air-defense grind — a role that burns through engines, parts, and missiles at a rate no peacetime sustainment plan contemplated.

Reuters framed the wider picture bluntly: Ukraine has, in essence, run out of the air-defense missiles that matter most. The F-16 fleet's troubles are one front in a broader air-defense crisis — one that Western capitals, well into the war's fourth year, have not solved. With winter approaching and Russia's drone and missile campaign showing no sign of easing, the pressure on the remaining flyable jets will only grow.
